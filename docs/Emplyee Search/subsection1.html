06 Search Strategy Design

"Every search algorithm is an approximation of human reasoning. Each succeeds under certain conditions and fails under others."

Once the employee records had been consolidated into a unified search model, the remaining challenge was identifying duplicate records despite inconsistencies in the underlying data.

Names were occasionally misspelled.

Some databases stored abbreviated forms.

Others contained typographical errors, inconsistent spacing, or variations in spelling accumulated over many years of manual data entry.

No single retrieval technique proved sufficiently robust to address every scenario.

Instead of searching for a single "best" algorithm, the project adopted a different philosophy.

Each algorithm was viewed as a specialised tool designed to solve a particular class of matching problems.

The overall search system therefore became a composition of complementary strategies rather than a dependence upon any individual technique.

This engineering decision significantly increased the probability of locating existing employee records while reducing the likelihood of overlooking duplicates.

6.1 Why One Search Algorithm Was Not Enough

Human names rarely conform to strict rules.

An employee may appear under slightly different spellings across independent databases.

Middle names may be omitted.

Spaces and punctuation may vary.

Typing mistakes are unavoidable.

Consequently, a search strategy relying solely upon exact matching inevitably fails whenever the stored representation differs from the user's input.

Conversely, search methods designed to tolerate variation often return excessive numbers of candidate records.

The engineering challenge therefore becomes balancing precision against recall.

Rather than attempting to maximise one at the expense of the other, the system deliberately employed several complementary search strategies.

Each compensated for weaknesses present in the others.

6.1 Exact Matching

Engineering Principle

The fastest search algorithm is often the least tolerant of imperfect data. Real-world engineering requires balancing computational efficiency against the variability of human-generated information.

Exact Matching

The simplest method of locating an employee record is an exact comparison against one or more database fields. This approach relies on SQL equality operators (=), allowing indexed columns such as employee codes or national identity numbers to be searched extremely efficiently.

For structured identifiers that are expected to be unique and correctly entered, exact matching remains the preferred technique. Queries execute rapidly, require minimal computational resources, and fully utilize database indexes.

However, employee information collected over many years rarely remains perfectly consistent. During the migration project, data originated from several independent systems developed by different vendors and maintained by different departments. As a result, even records representing the same employee frequently contained variations such as:

  • Differences in spelling
  • Additional spaces
  • Missing middle names
  • Abbreviations
  • Typographical mistakes
  • Different capitalization

  • Alternate punctuation

For example:

Muhammad Ali

Muhamad Ali

Muhammad  Ali

Muhammad A. Ali

Although these names clearly refer to the same individual, an exact comparison treats each value as completely different.

Consequently, exact matching was primarily used for fields whose values were expected to remain stable, including employee identifiers, CNIC numbers, or other unique codes. It served as the foundation of the search engine but was intentionally supplemented with progressively more tolerant search strategies capable of handling imperfect real-world data.

Strengths

  • Extremely fast execution
  • Fully indexable
  • Minimal CPU consumption
  • Ideal for unique identifiers

  • Predictable query plans

Limitations

  • No tolerance for spelling errors
  • Sensitive to whitespace and punctuation
  • Unable to detect duplicate records containing inconsistent text

  • Ineffective when users only remember part of a value
